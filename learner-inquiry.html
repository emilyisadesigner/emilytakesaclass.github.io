8.1 Student Inquiry Project

Promoting learner inquiry and divergent thinking in the library.

Published on 23 October 2026 by Emily Fox

Project Overview

Using a modified Agile methodology, high school students will investigate how the school library’s collection, spaces, and services currently support learners and report back to the librarian where they could work better. Students will explore the collection, gather feedback, generate possibilities, test a practical idea, and/or share a recommendation with library staff in a continuous iteration/continuous delivery (CI/CD) cadence. The project is designed for self-directed participation during lunch, study hall, before or after school, or library passes, and the content difficulty is recommended for grades 9-12. No scheduled lessons or direct instruction are required.

Driving Question

How might we make the school library’s collection, spaces, or services more useful, welcoming, and inspiring for the people who use them?

Learning Goals

Inquiry: Develop questions from observations, narrow an investigation, and use findings to make a recommendation.

Divergent thinking: Generate a wide range of ideas before selecting and refining the most valuable options.

Information literacy: Locate and evaluate relevant collection and user-feedback evidence; represent findings accurately and respectfully; learn the basics of a project management cadence.

Collaboration: Share responsibilities, listen to different perspectives, give feedback, and communicate group recommendations.

Program Resources

Students may visit at any time during open library access. Students can complete challenges individually or in groups, work asynchronously, and continue where they left off. Each phase’s instructions, prompts, templates, and checkpoint (when defined) are available through stations and the Google Classroom.

  • Stations will be placed in visible, accessible spots around the library. This will include a table nearest the door, the circulation desk, the back wall, and the study corrals/tables.
  • Google Classroom will serve as a hub for the project, including surveys, prompts, templates, the Kanban board, a Miro board for brainstorming, etc.
  • QR codes that link to Google Classroom will contain digital task cards, with short, self-explanatory steps, prompts, templates, and clear next actions. Students should be able to begin and continue without a staff-led explanation.
  • Project roadmap a Notion Kanban board where students can track progress and assign priority value to tasks.
  • Keep the staff role low-prep: restock station materials, offer light check-ins when students request them, and synthesize data into actionable steps (asynchronously).

Improvement Brainstorm Activities

50 Ideas

  1. Set a short timer or choose a manageable time window.
  2. Write or sketch as many possible library improvements as you can.
  3. Aim for variety; unusual ideas are welcome.
  4. Do not evaluate ideas until the round ends.

What if…?

  1. Choose a prompt from the Google Classroom list or write your own. Think about prompts such as: “What if a new student had only two minutes to find a book they would enjoy?” or “What if students could discover materials without browsing the shelves?”
  2. Generate several possible responses before choosing one to explore further.
  3. Outline 3-4 solutions to your prompt.
  4. Record ideas in a Google Classroom submission.

Yes-and

  1. Read or listen to a teammate’s idea.
  2. Add to it by beginning with “Yes, and…” Repeat until it gets silly.
  3. Write down the additions before discussing limitations.
  4. Build on multiple ideas before selecting a direction.
  5. Record final idea in a Google Classroom submission.

Brainstorm Reflection

The exit ticket will be in Google Classroom. Students must compare ideas using evidence, likely usefulness, inclusivity, cost, staff time, and ease of testing—not just personal preference. Record the reason for your choice.

Differentiation and Adaptation

Support different learners

  • Offer question stems, a sample audit, a visual project timeline, and clear task prompts.
  • Allow students to contribute through writing, drawing, speaking, organizing, interviewing, or making.
  • Provide accessible survey formats, such as a recorded entry.
  • Suggest that students choose a familiar or personally interesting area while encouraging them to consider users beyond themselves.
  • Provide a curated set of materials, source-evaluation prompts, sentence starters, or a smaller research scope when helpful.
  • Extend the project with a deeper review of catalog data, additional user feedback, multiple prototype rounds, or a cost-and-resource comparison.

8.1 Assignment Relection

The primary limitations for this project are all centered around the history of this library and its disuse. Students here are not accustomed to using the library or checking out books, or reading for pleasure at all. My activities are intended to engage our students in the improvement process, grant them a sense of agency, and build a personal investment in the space. I would love to see them engaging with the collection and finding materials they want to read. Currently I have 3 or so students who are volunteering in the library, and I intend to increase that number to 10+ by the end of the 2026-27 school year by further engaging them in the work and getting their peers involved. I’m relying heavily on the social connections of these students, which is a potential weakness, however, my connections with Student Services are helpful in identifying more students with an interest in reading or the library who I can invite to participate.

I am most excited about seeing what these kids come up with! I want to create a space that serves them and meets them where they are. Aside from some big changes, like getting paint up on the walls and creating a comfortable seating area, I don’t have a clue what they really need to be comfortable in this library. Opening the door to the day-to-day wants of the students is going to be really fun, and I hope we get a nice spread of actionable ideas.

Toolkit

  • Notion — SAMR: Augmentation.

    By hosting a Kanban board in a central digital hub, students will be able to see the progress of their ideas in action and contribute meaningfully to the prioritization and backlog.

    3 Minute Tutorial: How To Create A Kanban Board In Notion (2026 Easy Guide)

  • Miro — SAMR: Redefinition.

    Using a collaborative whiteboard for brainstorming will help visual learners as we map user feedback, build journey maps, or redesign a library floor plan together. Students and other approved contributors can co-create and remix a shared redesign across visits, enabling a form of community collaboration that would have been difficult to accomplish in the same way with paper alone.

  • Canva — SAMR: Augmentation.

    Use Canva to design signage, display posters, and prototype digital pitch materials. Digital editing, reusable layouts, and accessible design features improve the creation task while keeping it close to familiar poster and signage work. Canva Pro is also free for our students and educators, giving them a wider range of design options without resorting to AI.

  • Google Forms — SAMR: Modification.

    Google Forms will be used through the Library Google Classroom to create and share surveys, then review responses to identify patterns. Digital collection and summaries can substantially change how feedback is gathered and analyzed; keeping it within the school-approved tools means we will use only approved settings and avoid collecting identifying or sensitive information.

Digital Citizenship and GenAI in the Learning Environment

Published on 2 October 2026 by Emily Fox

Digital Citizenship

My circumstances mean that I haven’t witnessed much recent teaching of DC in my time at this school, but when I was an Ed Tech III in the day treatment program, the lead teacher was clearly conscious of the need to be teaching it when and however possible. As part of one of her classes I advocated for a digital privacy lesson which I taught with the help of free online resources (the name of which I can’t remember right now). I had the students look through the social media profiles of three (fake) high school students and use the public information available to fill out questionnaires about where they attended school, their birth dates, full legal names, town or address, contact information, and more. I found this exercise to be fun for both the students and myself, and it was a low-lift lesson with a lot of room to expand and reflect on the results, especially given that it was student-driven and required them to do this “detective work” to reach their own conclusions. It was a small step, but it contained elements of digital security, literacy, health and wellness, and etiquette. I don’t currently have the opportunity to teach this lesson again, but as part of my role in the library I feel a responsibility to share safe and sane DC practices with the students, and I intend to work that into my role when I’m more established.

Many kids aren’t getting digital citizenship education at home, and like Turkle (2010) describes, it’s apparent even sixteen years later that parents being on their own devices rather than connecting with their children continues to have a negative effect on their social-emotional development. Whether the lack of education stems from parent ability, parent willingness or awareness, or from simple oversight, I see kids here engaging in unsafe online practices all the time. At the very least we can serve as a safety net, but it’s more likely that teaching DC as part of the school curriculum at every level will go a long way towards keeping our communities safer. I believe that it should be an inescapable lesson, the responsibility of which is shared across disciplines and grade levels – not a class that students might miss or zone out of. By embedding these teachings into everyday lessons (“I’m emailing you this file; do you know how to recognize a legitimate email from a phishing scam?”) students will hopefully remember to pause and think about it each time they choose to share part of themselves online.

GenAI

I strongly believe that everyone should be deeply educated on how AI models have been created and trained, their inescapable bias, the real, current dangers of cognitive atrophy, and the environmental and economic impacts of their existence. There is no ethical way to interact with AI, so every step we take towards its integration is a concession we’re making on a moral level. Any investment made towards GenAI should be in the form of that education about it, encouraging critical thinking in the student population, and limiting the actual use of it in the classroom.

While I do use GenAI tools (Claude specifically), I have fairly strict personal guidelines of when and what I use it for. I opt to use it primarily as an LLM and not to generate images, long form writing, or content. There are gray areas within the gray areas, because I’ve used it to create sub plans at 5am when I was too sick to think, and to generate a capsule wardrobe list that fits my lifestyle. Most often I’m using it as a comparative search tool so that I can analyze the results myself.

Interestingly, I feel that search engines missed the mark in a huge way when it comes to AI integration. There is so much advertising bloat in every Google search that when I was looking for the right backpack at the end of last school year, I abandoned Google entirely in favor of inputting my extensive specifications into Claude and shopping out of the results. To me, that is a missed opportunity in the search engine space; all I’m asking for is a logic puzzle, a compilation of yes/no statements about items posted on the internet. While at its core this is a GenAI function, it could have been implemented by search engines, and I wish that it had been. I don’t have the research to know if its use would still require massive cooling centers, or if that's a given for any LLM or AI generation, but it feels as though it would have a smaller impact than entire companies providing that service and more.

As far as allowing learners to use GenAI at school? Luckily, it isn’t up to me.
